2  Hilbert space for random vectors

2.1 Learning Objectives

  • Work with mean-zero, square-integrable random functions
  • Interpret orthogonality through covariance
  • Use the projection theorem in statistical problems

2.2 Chapter Summary

This chapter introduces the Hilbert space of \(q\)-dimensional random functions \(h(Z)\) with mean zero and finite second moments:

\[ E\{h(Z)\} = 0, \qquad E\{h^\top(Z)h(Z)\} < \infty. \]

The covariance inner product is defined by

\[ \langle h_1, h_2\rangle = E(h_1^\top h_2), \]

with corresponding norm

\[ \|h\| = \langle h,h\rangle^{1/2}. \]

Two elements are orthogonal when their inner product is zero. For a closed linear subspace \(\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{H}\), the projection theorem guarantees a unique projection \(\Pi(h\mid\mathcal{U})\in\mathcal{U}\) such that

\[ \Pi(h\mid\mathcal{U}) = \arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}} \|h-u\|. \]

The residual after projection is orthogonal to the subspace:

\[ h-\Pi(h\mid\mathcal{U}) \perp \mathcal{U}. \]

For a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by a random vector \(u(Z)\), the projection is a linear function of \(u\), whose coefficients are determined by the variance of \(u(Z)\) and its covariance with \(h(Z)\) (see (2.1) below).

2.3 Notes and Extensions

2.3.1 Projection onto linear span vs conditional expectation

By definition,

\[ \Pi\{h(Z)\mid\mathcal{U}\} = \arg\min_{g\in\mathcal{U}}\|h-g\|, \]

for any closed linear subspace \(\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{H}\). That is, the projection of \(h(Z)\) onto \(\mathcal{U}\) is the element of \(\mathcal{U}\) closest to \(h(Z)\) in the norm \(\|\cdot\|\).

Example 1 in Tsiatis (2006) considers the linear span

\[ \mathcal{U} = \left\{ a^{\top}u : a\in\mathbb{R}^k \right\} \]

of a \(k\)-dimensional random vector

\[ u(Z) = \{u_1(Z),\ldots,u_k(Z)\}^{\top}. \]

It establishes that

\[ \Pi\{h(Z)\mid\mathcal{U}\} = E(hu^{\top}) \left\{ E(uu^{\top}) \right\}^{-1} u. \tag{2.1}\]

If \(\mathcal{U}\) is expanded to include all measurable square-integrable functions of \(u(Z)\), the projection is the conditional expectation of \(h(Z)\) given \(u(Z)\) (Tsiatis, 2006). Specifically,

\[ E\{h(Z)\mid u\} = \Pi\{h(Z)\mid\mathcal{U}^*\} = \arg\min_{g\in\mathcal{U}^*} \|h-g\|, \]

where

\[ \mathcal{U}^* = \left\{ a(u) : a(\cdot) \text{ is a measurable and square-integrable function on } \mathbb{R}^k \right\}. \]

Because \(\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{U}^*\), the best approximation of \(h(Z)\) in \(\mathcal{U}^*\) is at least as good as the best approximation in \(\mathcal{U}\). Thus,

\[ \left\| h-E\{h(Z)\mid u\} \right\| \leq \left\| h-\Pi\{h(Z)\mid\mathcal{U}\} \right\|. \]

By a standard property of the multivariate normal distribution, \(E\{h(Z)\mid u\}\) is linear in \(u\) when \(h(Z)\) and \(u(Z)\) are jointly multivariate normal. In this case,

\[ E\{h(Z)\mid u\} = \Pi\{h(Z)\mid\mathcal{U}\}, \] which is in turn given by (2.1).

2.3.2 Dimensionality of \(\mathcal{U}=\{Bv:B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}\}\)

Here, \(v\) is an \(r\)-dimensional random vector. On page 17, it is claimed that \(\mathcal{U}\) is \(q\times r\)-dimensional because it is the linear span of that many linearly independent functions.

Indeed, a typical element of \(\mathcal{U}\) is given by \[ \begin{aligned} u = Bv = \begin{pmatrix} \sum_{j=1}^r B_{1j}v_j \\ \sum_{j=1}^r B_{2j}v_j \\ \vdots \\ \sum_{j=1}^r B_{qj}v_j \end{pmatrix} = \sum_{j=1}^r \begin{pmatrix} B_{1j}v_j \\ B_{2j}v_j \\ \vdots \\ B_{qj}v_j \end{pmatrix} = \sum_{j=1}^r\sum_{i=1}^q B_{ij} u_{ij}^{q\times 1}, \end{aligned} \]

where \[ u_{ij}^{q\times 1} = (\underbrace{0,\ldots, 0}_{i-1}， v_j, \underbrace{0， \ldots, 0}_{q-i})^\top, \] as defined in the book.

2.4 Solutions to Exercises

2.4.1 Exercise 2.1

Solution. Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be a closed linear subspace of the Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\), and let \(h\in\mathcal{H}\). We prove:

  • Existence: There exists \(u_0\in\mathcal{U}\) such that

    \[ \|h-u_0\| = \inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}} \|h-u\|. \]

  • Uniqueness: The minimizer \(u_0\) is unique.

  • Orthogonality: The residual is orthogonal to \(\mathcal{U}\):

    \[ \langle h-u_0,u\rangle = 0 \qquad \text{for every } u\in\mathcal{U}. \]

To show existence, let

\[ d = \inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}} \|h-u\|. \]

Choose a sequence \(\{u_n\}\subset\mathcal{U}\) such that

\[ \|h-u_n\| \longrightarrow d. \]

We first show that \(\{u_n\}\) is a Cauchy sequence. Because \((u_n+u_m)/2\in\mathcal{U}\),

\[ d \leq \left\| h-\frac{u_n+u_m}{2} \right\|. \]

Moreover,

\[ \begin{aligned} \|u_n-u_m\|^2 &= \left\| (h-u_n)-(h-u_m) \right\|^2 \\ &= \|h-u_n\|^2 + \|h-u_m\|^2 - 2\langle h-u_n,h-u_m\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} 4d^2 &\leq 4\left\| h-\frac{u_n+u_m}{2} \right\|^2 \\ &= \left\| (h-u_n)+(h-u_m) \right\|^2 \\ &= \|h-u_n\|^2 + \|h-u_m\|^2 + 2\langle h-u_n,h-u_m\rangle \\ &= 2\|h-u_n\|^2 + 2\|h-u_m\|^2 - \|u_n-u_m\|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Hence,

\[ \begin{aligned} \|u_n-u_m\|^2 &\leq 2\|h-u_n\|^2 + 2\|h-u_m\|^2 - 4d^2 \\ &\longrightarrow 0 \qquad \text{as } n,m\longrightarrow\infty. \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \(\{u_n\}\) is Cauchy. Since \(\mathcal{H}\) is complete, there exists \(u_0\in\mathcal{H}\) such that

\[ u_n\longrightarrow u_0. \]

Because \(\mathcal{U}\) is closed and \(u_n\in\mathcal{U}\) for every \(n\),

\[ u_0\in\mathcal{U}. \]

By continuity of the norm,

\[ \|h-u_0\| = \lim_{n\to\infty}\|h-u_n\| = d. \]

This establishes existence.

To show orthogonality, fix \(u\in\mathcal{U}\) and define

\[ g(\epsilon) = \|h-(u_0-\epsilon u)\|^2. \]

Since \(u_0-\epsilon u\in\mathcal{U}\) for every \(\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}\), \(g(\epsilon)\) is minimized at \(\epsilon=0\). Hence,

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon} g(\epsilon) \right|_{\epsilon=0} \\ &= 2\langle h-u_0,u\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \langle h-u_0,u\rangle=0 \qquad \text{for every } u\in\mathcal{U}. \]

Finally, for uniqueness, suppose that \(u_0\) and \(u_0^*\) both minimize \(\|h-u\|\) over \(\mathcal{U}\). Since \(u_0-u_0^*\in\mathcal{U}\), orthogonality gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \|h-u_0^*\|^2 &= \underbrace{\|h-u_0\|^2}_{d^2} + \underbrace{\|u_0-u_0^*\|^2}_{\geq 0}. \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\|h-u_0^*\|^2=d^2\), it follows that

\[ \|u_0-u_0^*\|^2=0, \]

and hence \(u_0=u_0^*\).

Solution. This exercise is a direct application of Example 2 in the book.

  1. Because \(\Sigma_{22}=E(Y_2Y_2^{\mathsf T})\) is nonsingular, the projection formula in Example 2 gives \[ \begin{aligned} \Pi(Y_1\mid\mathcal{U}) &= E(Y_1Y_2^{\mathsf T}) \left\{ E(Y_2Y_2^{\mathsf T}) \right\}^{-1} Y_2 \\ &= \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}Y_2, \end{aligned} \] which is equal to \(E(Y_1\mid Y_2)\) (see Section 2.3.1).

  2. Thus, the residual is \[ Y_1-\Pi(Y_1\mid\mathcal{U}) = Y_1-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}Y_2. \] Its squared norm is

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\| Y_1-\Pi(Y_1\mid\mathcal{U}) \right\|^2 &= \operatorname{tr} \left( \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \right). \end{aligned} \]

2.4.3 Exercise 2.3

Solution. Let

\[ W = Z_1^2-\sigma_1^2. \]

Then

\[ \mathcal{U} = \left\{ a_1W+a_2Z_2 : a_1,a_2\in\mathbb{R} \right\}. \]

Because \((Z_1,Z_2)^{\mathsf T}\) is mean-zero multivariate normal, all third moments are zero. Thus,

\[ \begin{aligned} E(Z_1W) &= E(Z_1^3)-\sigma_1^2E(Z_1) = 0, \end{aligned} \]

and

\[ \begin{aligned} E(WZ_2) &= E(Z_1^2Z_2)-\sigma_1^2E(Z_2) = 0, \end{aligned} \] where we have used \[ E(Z_1^2Z_2) = E\{Z_2\underbrace{E(Z_1^2\mid Z_2)}_{\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_2^{-2}\sigma_{12}^2}\} = (\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_2^{-2}\sigma_{12}^2)E(Z_2) = 0. \]

Thus, \(W\) is orthogonal to both \(Z_1\) and \(Z_2\). This means that the problem essentially reduces to projecting \(Z_1\) onto the linear span of \(Z_2\) alone.

  1. The component of \(Z_1\) in the direction of \(W\) is zero. Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pi(Z_1\mid\mathcal{U}) &= \frac{E(Z_1Z_2)}{E(Z_2^2)}Z_2 = \sigma_{2}^{-2}\sigma_{12}Z_2. \end{aligned} \]

  1. The residual is \[ Z_1-\Pi(Z_1\mid\mathcal{U}) = Z_1-\sigma_{2}^{-2}\sigma_{12}Z_2. \] Its variance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{var} \left\{ Z_1-\Pi(Z_1\mid\mathcal{U}) \right\} &= \sigma_1^2 - \sigma_{2}^{-2}\sigma_{12}^2. \end{aligned} \]

2.5 Additional Problems

Problem 2.1

Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be a closed linear subspace of \(\mathcal{H}\), and let

\[ u_0=\Pi(h\mid\mathcal{U}). \]

Show that, for every \(u\in\mathcal{U}\),

\[ \|h-u\|^2 = \|h-u_0\|^2 + \|u-u_0\|^2. \]

Problem 2.2

Let \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), and consider the Hilbert space of one-dimensional mean-zero functions of \(Z\) with finite variance. Let

\[ \mathcal{U} = \operatorname{span} \left\{ Z,\, Z^2-1 \right\}. \]

  1. Show that \(Z\) and \(Z^2-1\) are orthogonal.

  2. Find \(\Pi(Z^3\mid\mathcal{U})\).

  3. Compute the variance of

\[ Z^3-\Pi(Z^3\mid\mathcal{U}). \]

Problem 2.3

Let \(h_1,h_2\in\mathcal{H}\). For any real number \(a\), use

\[ \|h_1-ah_2\|^2\geq 0 \]

to prove the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\[ \left| \langle h_1,h_2\rangle \right|^2 \leq \|h_1\|^2\|h_2\|^2. \]

Show that equality holds if and only if \(h_1=ah_2\) for some scalar \(a\).

Problem 2.4

Let \(Z\) have support \(\{z_1,z_2,z_3\}\), with positive probabilities \(\pi_1,\pi_2,\pi_3\).

  1. Find two linearly independent mean-zero functions of \(Z\) that span the space of all one-dimensional mean-zero functions of \(Z\).

  2. What is the dimension of the space of all \(q\)-dimensional mean-zero functions of \(Z\)?

### Exercise 2.1

::: {.solution}
Let $\mathcal{U}$ be a closed linear subspace of the Hilbert space
$\mathcal{H}$, and let $h\in\mathcal{H}$. We prove:

- **Existence**: There exists $u_0\in\mathcal{U}$ such that

   $$
   \|h-u_0\|
   =
   \inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}}
   \|h-u\|.
   $$

- **Orthogonality**:  The residual is orthogonal to $\mathcal{U}$:

   $$
   \langle h-u_0,u\rangle = 0
   \qquad
   \text{for every } u\in\mathcal{U}.
   $$

To show **existence**, let

$$
d
=
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}}
\|h-u\|.
$$

Choose a sequence $\{u_n\}\subset\mathcal{U}$ such that

$$
\|h-u_n\| \longrightarrow d.
$$

We first show that $\{u_n\}$ is a Cauchy sequence. Because $(u_n+u_m)/2\in\mathcal{U}$,

$$
d
\leq
\left\|
h-\frac{u_n+u_m}{2}
\right\|.
$$

Moreover,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|u_n-u_m\|^2
&=
\left\|
(h-u_n)-(h-u_m)
\right\|^2 \\
&=
\|h-u_n\|^2
+
\|h-u_m\|^2
-
2\langle h-u_n,h-u_m\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
4d^2
&\leq
4\left\|
h-\frac{u_n+u_m}{2}
\right\|^2 \\
&=
\left\|
(h-u_n)+(h-u_m)
\right\|^2 \\
&=
\|h-u_n\|^2
+
\|h-u_m\|^2
+
2\langle h-u_n,h-u_m\rangle \\
&=
2\|h-u_n\|^2
+
2\|h-u_m\|^2
-
\|u_n-u_m\|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|u_n-u_m\|^2
&\leq
2\|h-u_n\|^2
+
2\|h-u_m\|^2
-
4d^2 \\
&\longrightarrow 0
\qquad
\text{as } n,m\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, $\{u_n\}$ is Cauchy. Since $\mathcal{H}$ is complete, there exists
$u_0\in\mathcal{H}$ such that

$$
u_n\longrightarrow u_0.
$$

Because $\mathcal{U}$ is closed and $u_n\in\mathcal{U}$ for every $n$,

$$
u_0\in\mathcal{U}.
$$

By continuity of the norm,

$$
\|h-u_0\|
=
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|h-u_n\|
=
d.
$$

This establishes existence.

To show **orthogonality**, fix $u\in\mathcal{U}$ and define

$$
g(\epsilon)
=
\|h-(u_0-\epsilon u)\|^2.
$$

Since $u_0-\epsilon u\in\mathcal{U}$ for every $\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}$,
$g(\epsilon)$ is minimized at $\epsilon=0$. Hence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
0
&=
\left.
\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}
g(\epsilon)
\right|_{\epsilon=0} \\
&=
2\langle h-u_0,u\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\langle h-u_0,u\rangle=0
\qquad
\text{for every } u\in\mathcal{U}.
$$

Finally, for **uniqueness**, suppose that $u_0$ and $u_0^*$ both minimize $\|h-u\|$ over
$\mathcal{U}$. Since $u_0-u_0^*\in\mathcal{U}$, orthogonality gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|h-u_0^*\|^2
&=
\underbrace{\|h-u_0\|^2}_{d^2}
+
\underbrace{\|u_0-u_0^*\|^2}_{\geq 0}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $\|h-u_0^*\|^2=d^2$, it follows that

$$
\|u_0-u_0^*\|^2=0,
$$

and hence $u_0=u_0^*$.

Its variance is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{var}
\left\{
Z_1-\Pi(Z_1\mid\mathcal{U})
\right\}
&=
\sigma_1^2
-
\sigma_{2}^{-2}\sigma_{12}^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

\mathcal{U}
=

\left\{

Z^2-1
\right\}.

### Problem 2.3 {.unnumbered}

Show that equality holds if and only if $h_1=ah_2$ for some scalar $a$.
